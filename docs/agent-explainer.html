1The challenge: video games with the instruction manual torn out

ARC-AGI-3 is a set of small puzzle video games. A human can pick one up and figure out the rules in a minute. The catch for a computer: there are no instructions, no score labels, no hints — just a picture on screen and a handful of buttons. The agent has to discover the rules by experimenting, then solve the puzzle, the way a curious child pokes at a new toy until it "gets it."

Analogy. Imagine being dropped in front of an arcade cabinet in a country whose language you can't read. The screen shows colored blocks; there are five buttons and a touch-screen. Nobody tells you the goal. You press things, watch what changes, form a theory ("ah, this button moves the little guy"), and keep going until the screen flashes "level complete." That is exactly the agent's situation — thousands of times, fast.

Each game has several levels that get harder. Winning a game means clearing all its levels. The real test uses hidden games the agent has never seen — so it cannot memorize answers; it must have genuinely general skills.

2What the agent sees, and what it can do

It sees a grid of colored squares

Every moment, the game hands the agent one frame: a 64×64 grid where each square is one of 16 colors. That's it — the same thing you'd see on screen, just as numbers. Here's a tiny made-up frame: a "player" (blue), two "walls" (grey), a "goal" (green), and a background (dark).

The agent is never told "blue = player" or "green = goal." It has to work that out by moving and noticing which colored blob moves when I press a button.

It has a tiny set of buttons

  • 1 2 3 4 — usually move up / down / left / right (but in some games they do other things).
  • 5 — a "context" button: in different games it might submit an answer, select, or fire.
  • click — tap a specific square by its (x, y) position. Many games are click-based (press a button, place a tile, toggle a switch).
  • reset — start the current game over from the beginning.
The single most important quirk: the agent can hit reset as often as it likes, and the games are perfectly predictable (do the same thing → get the same result). This turns out to be a superpower — it means the agent can rehearse. More on that in section 4.

3How points work — and why "fast & clean" beats "eventually"

You'd think the score is just "how many levels did you clear." It's subtler, and understanding it is the key to everything the agent does. Three rules:

① Efficiency, squared

Each level's score compares your moves to a human's moves. Using the same or fewer moves than a human ≈ full marks. Using twice as many ≈ a quarter of the marks (it's the ratio squared). Sloppy wins are punished hard.

② Deep levels count more

Later levels are weighted more heavily than early ones. Clearing level 6 efficiently is worth far more than clearing level 1 efficiently.

③ The game ends the instant you fully win — and you keep your best attempt

The moment one run clears every level, the game stops. But along the way the agent can make several separate attempts (it resets between them), and only your best-scoring attempt counts.

Analogy — a diving competition. You get several dives; only your best dive scores (rule ③, "keep your best attempt"). A clean, precise dive scores far more than a splashy one that technically reached the water (rule ①, efficiency-squared). And harder dives carry a bigger difficulty multiplier (rule ②, deep levels count more). A messy belly-flop that happens to finish first can actually end your session with a low score before you get to show a clean dive — that exact trap shaped our biggest design decision (section 8).
Why this matters — a real number
On one game (codename tu93), clearing all 9 levels by wandering around scored 0.3 out of 100. Clearing the same levels efficiently scored ~54 out of 100 — same levels, ~170× the score. Efficiency is not a nice-to-have; it's almost the whole game.

4The big idea: "rehearse messily, then perform cleanly" (search-replay)

Because the games are predictable and resettable (section 2), the agent doesn't need a crystal ball. It uses a two-phase trick called search-replay:

A
Search (the messy rehearsal). Poke around, reset, try things, reset again — a chaotic exploration whose only job is to find a sequence of moves that reaches the win. This attempt scores terribly (lots of wasted moves), and that's fine — we'll throw it away.
↓
B
Replay (the clean performance). Start a fresh attempt and play back the shortest version of the solution it found — no wandering. Because the game is predictable, the clean replay is guaranteed to work, and it scores high.
Analogy. A stunt driver walks the course on foot first, trying routes and hitting cones (rehearsal — nobody's timing it). Then, for the filmed take, they drive the one clean line they found, fast and flawless. The agent's "filmed take" is the replay; the reset button is what lets it walk the course as many times as it needs.

This sidesteps the hardest problem in AI — building an accurate mental model of a world you don't understand. The agent doesn't need to understand the game; it just needs to find a path in the real game and then retrace it. The real game is the model.

5Not one brain — a team of 14 specialists

There is no single algorithm. The agent is a portfolio: a line-up of 14 different strategies (we call each a "play"). On a given game they take turns. Each one is its own attempt, and — remember rule ③ — the best-scoring attempt wins. Two principles hold the team together:

Keep-the-best ("max-over-plays")

Since only your best attempt counts, adding a new specialist can only help: if it does better on some game, great; if it does worse, its attempt is simply ignored.

Never-make-it-worse ("floor-safety")

Every specialist is designed to bow out gracefully ("abstain") on games it doesn't understand, doing nothing harmful. So the team's known abilities are a protected floor that new additions can't break.

Analogy. A pub-quiz team. You bring a history buff, a sports nut, a movie geek… For each question, whoever knows it answers; the others stay quiet. Adding another specialist never lowers your score — it only adds questions you might now get right. Our agent is that team, and the "questions" are different puzzle games.

6Meet every specialist (in the order they play)

They run roughly from "sharp, specific solvers" first to "broad, brute-force searchers" and finally the "catch-all wanderer" last. Here's each one in plain terms, with an example of the kind of game it handles.

1
Cheap "shape" solvers — peg / centroid-drag / pull-drag
quick specialists
Three classic puzzle patterns it can recognise instantly from the picture, with zero guessing. Peg-solitaire: jump pieces over each other to remove them until one remains. Centroid-drag / pull-drag: grab a piece and drag it to a target spot. If the current game doesn't look like any of these, this play gives up in a single step (so it costs almost nothing).
Example: a board of evenly-spaced dots — it recognises "peg-solitaire," works out the legal jumps, and clears the board directly, no exploration needed.
2
Geodesic replay — the efficiency engine
the score-maker
This is the star player for points. It explores to find the rewards, then computes the shortest possible path to each reward (a "geodesic" = the straight-line route through the maze of game states) and replays that in a clean run. This is the search-replay idea (section 4) turned into a reliable machine. It's why efficiency-squared scoring works in our favour.
Example: the wandering rehearsal took 431 moves to clear a level; the geodesic replay does the same level in 18 — a 24× cut that the squared scoring turns into a huge point gain.
3
Transfer explorers (several variants)
learn once, reuse
Levels of one game share an escalating mechanic. When a level is solved, the winning action is a genuine labelled example of "what works here." These plays carry that lesson forward to later levels instead of rediscovering it from scratch — and some variants use a more abstract view of the board (relationships between objects, not exact pixels) to push into deeper levels with huge, confusing screens.
Example: level 1 taught "step onto the yellow tile to score." Level 4's screen looks different, but the transfer play already suspects yellow tiles matter — so it heads there first.
4
Chain-macro replay
reuse the whole recipe
Where the transfer plays carry a hint, this one remembers the entire sequence of clicks that solved the last level and tries replaying the recipe on the next one — since later levels are often "the same trick, bigger."
Example: a game where each level is "click the buttons in this pattern" — once learned, the pattern replays with tiny tweaks rather than being re-searched.
5
Archetype solvers — mechanic / grab-drag / paint / glyph
named-trick experts
Four hand-crafted experts for recognisable game "archetypes," each written to solve its trick directly and efficiently (no wandering) — and to abstain on anything else: mechanic pattern-match / arrangement puzzles; grab-drag steer an avatar that drags a block onto a target; paint drag a "brush" so its trail fills a stencil to match a template; glyph read a symbol, toggle matching switches, then walk to the goal.
Example (paint): the target is an outlined shape; the expert picks the right-colored piece, then drags its cursor so the trail colors exactly that shape — like coloring inside the lines.
6
General agent — the universal searcher
works on anything
The game-agnostic fallback searcher. It first probes what actions even do something ("does clicking here change anything? does clicking A then B do something new?"), then runs a smart exploration that prefers unvisited situations, until it stumbles into a win — then replays it cleanly. It needs no knowledge of the specific game, so it can crack brand-new hidden games.
Example: a game none of the specialists recognise — the general agent still finds a path to the first reward by systematic trial-and-reset, and banks it.
7
Object-centric search (oc_search) — added this project
smarter clicking
A twin of the general searcher with two upgrades: it aims clicks at the centres of actual objects (not random pixels), and it recognises two screens as "the same" based on the objects present rather than exact pixels — which stops flickering animations from exploding its search. This cracked several games the plain searcher missed.
Example (ka59): the winning move is clicking the exact centre of a small block. Random-pixel clicking never hits it; object-centre clicking solves the level in ~11 moves.
8
Coverage anchor (moved to LAST this project)
safety net
A patient, general "wanderer" that will eventually clear many games by brute persistence — but sloppily. It's the last resort: it guarantees the team still solves a game nothing else could, banking coverage as a floor. Why last? See section 8 — putting it first was quietly costing us most of our points.
Example: a game with an unfamiliar mechanic that only yields to stubborn trial-and-error — the anchor gets it done so we score something, even if not efficiently.

7Giving the agent "eyes": turning pixels into objects

Most specialists don't reason over raw squares — that's like reading a book one ink-dot at a time. A perception layer first turns the 64×64 grid into objects: it groups touching squares of the same color into blobs, and notes each blob's size, shape and centre.

→
Left: raw colored squares. Right: the agent's view — a handful of labelled objects ("a 1-square player here, an L-shaped wall there, a goal blob there"). Far easier to reason about.

It also spots the agent (the object that moves when you press a move button) and masks the HUD — flickering score-bars or timers at the screen edge that change constantly but don't matter. Ignoring that flicker keeps the agent from thinking every frame is a brand-new situation.

Analogy. When you watch a game you don't consciously track pixels — you see "my guy," "a wall," "the exit," and you tune out the timer ticking in the corner. The perception layer gives the agent that same at-a-glance understanding.

8Two clever tricks that unlocked most of the points

Trick 1 — put the sloppy wanderer last (efficiency-first)

Recall rule ③: the game ends the instant any attempt fully wins. Our patient wanderer used to go first. On games it could solve, it would grind out a win — sloppily — which ended the game before the efficient specialists ever got their turn. We were, in effect, always submitting the belly-flop.

The fix (this project): move the wanderer to last. Now the sharp, efficient specialists play first and produce the winning run; the wanderer only steps in for games nothing else can solve. Measured across 25 practice games, this nearly doubled our efficiency score (+81%) with zero games getting worse. The efficiency was always there — it was just being cut off before it could show.

Trick 2 — reset twice (the "double-reset")

We discovered the game engine has a subtle quirk: after you clear a deep level, a single reset cleans up the picture but leaves a hidden "you already finished a level" flag stuck on. The next run then reports phantom wins that aren't real. Pressing reset twice fully wipes the slate.

Analogy. Like a stopwatch that clears its display on one press but keeps a lap stored in memory — you have to press twice to truly zero it. Every clean "performance" run in the agent starts with a double-reset so it's genuinely starting fresh.

(This one mattered for honesty as much as points: a single reset produced fake "wins" during testing that would have fooled us — catching it kept our results real.)

9Putting it together: one game, start to finish

1
The game hands over the first frame. Perception turns it into objects; the agent notes what's on screen.
↓
2
Specialist #1 looks: "Is this a peg / drag puzzle I recognise?" If yes, it solves it efficiently and we're likely done. If no, it abstains in a single step.
↓
3
The turn passes down the line — geodesic (find rewards, replay the shortest path), then transfer / chain-macro (reuse lessons), then the archetype experts, then the general & object-centric searchers. Each is a fresh attempt begun with a double-reset.
↓
4
Whenever an attempt clears every level, the game ends. Across all attempts, the best-scoring one is what counts — so the sharp specialists' clean runs are what get recorded.
↓
5
If nothing efficient cracked it, the coverage anchor (last) grinds out a win so we still score. And through all of this, a fail-safe wrapper guarantees the agent always makes a legal move — even if something crashes, it falls back to random rather than freezing.

All of this runs offline on the competition's computers, with a generous time budget per game (about 8 hours each, run in parallel), talking to the game through a simple request-response channel.

10What it's good at — and where it still struggles (honestly)

Strong at

  • Games that are predictable and resettable — its whole rehearse-then-perform trick relies on this.
  • Efficiency on levels it can find a path to (the geodesic engine).
  • Recognisable archetypes (peg, drag, paint, glyph, pattern-match) — solved directly.
  • Not breaking: new abilities can only add; a fail-safe guarantees it always acts.

Still hard

  • Deep efficiency: it clears early levels efficiently but often clears the deepest levels sloppily — the next big lever we're chasing.
  • Truly novel mechanics in hidden games: if a game's trick is unlike anything the specialists know, only the slow searcher can attempt it, and sometimes it can't.
  • Games with hidden state (things you can't see on screen) — you can't rehearse what you can't observe.
The one-sentence summary. The agent is a team of puzzle specialists that rehearses in a resettable game until it finds a clean solution, keeps only its best attempt, and — after a fix we made this project — lets its most efficient specialists go first so the score reflects clean solutions, not lucky messy ones.
